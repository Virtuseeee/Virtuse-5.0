Who Actually Controls Your Money?

A banking failure in Paraguay, Bitcoin ETF outflows and the digital euro bring the same question into focus: who gets the final say over your money?

By Ras Vasilisin Markets desk 9 Nov 2026 · 09:56 UTC Block — 4 min
Share

Week of 2–8 November 2026. Reporting checked on Sunday, 8 November 2026 (Europe/Bratislava); ETF figures cover the five U.S. trading sessions ending 6 November.

You did the work. You earned the money. So why are you waiting for permission to use your own money?

Today we start in Paraguay, where that question stopped being theoretical.

I find it easy to focus on the price and forget the more basic question: can you actually use what you own?

This week gave us a banking failure, another ugly stretch for Bitcoin ETFs, a step closer to the digital euro and a change of political weather in Washington. Different headlines. The same question underneath: who gets the final say over your money?

A balance on a screen is not the same as access

Nearly three million customers were left unable to access their accounts after Paraguay’s Ueno Bank was ordered into resolution, according to The Guardian’s reporting on 7 November.

Some deposit-guarantee payments had begun by Friday. That is not the same as everyone getting their money back when they need it. Bills do not wait for a resolution process.

This is where Bitcoin’s self-custody argument becomes practical. If you control the private keys, you can authorise a transaction without asking a bank to reopen your account.

It does not mean Bitcoin would have spared those families every problem. A falling price can wreck a budget too. So can a stolen recovery phrase. Holding your keys removes a particular dependency; it does not remove risk.

Before moving anything, understand the setup and test your backup. Virtuse’s hardware-wallet comparison is a place to start. A device in a drawer is not a recovery plan.

Wall Street’s door opens both ways

U.S. spot Bitcoin ETFs recorded $1.282 billion in net outflows across 2–6 November, based on Farside Investors’ daily figures. Four of the five sessions were negative. Wednesday’s $74.6 million inflow barely interrupted the selling.

An ETF is a convenient way to get price exposure. It is not the same as holding spendable bitcoin under your own keys. Neither choice should be made by accident.

The digital euro will still be a euro

In his 5 November speech, the ECB’s Piero Cipollone confirmed plans for a 12-month digital-euro pilot starting in the second half of 2027, involving 36 payment providers.

A possible first issuance in 2029 remains conditional on legislation and a later ECB decision. A pilot is not a public launch.

There is a reasonable case for a European payment network less dependent on foreign card companies. But a new payment system does not turn the euro into Bitcoin. The issuer still sets the monetary framework.

Privacy deserves scrutiny, not made-up horror stories. The ECB’s proposed design includes cash-like privacy for offline payments and rules against programmable money. Online payment providers would still have identification obligations.

Read the safeguards. Then watch whether the final rules keep them.

Washington changed. Your backup still needs checking

The Democrats’ midterm sweep changes the prospects for U.S. Bitcoin legislation. It does not automatically replace the leadership of the SEC and CFTC.

Swan founder Cory Klippsten’s response, summarised in Bitcoin Breakdown’s 6 November edition, was to focus on self-custody and sound money rather than strategic-reserve promises from Washington.

That is the part worth keeping. Policy matters for access, taxes and businesses. But a politician’s promise is a poor substitute for a plan you can carry out yourself.

Corporate buying power comes with conditions

Strategy’s STRC preferred shares returned to their $100 par value on 4 November, after roughly $2.25 billion in buybacks. November’s annualised dividend rate is 12%, with payments moving to each business day, as described in the SEC filing.

Trading around par matters because it can reopen a route for issuing shares to fund Bitcoin purchases. It is not proof that fresh purchases have already happened. That route still needs willing buyers, and the dividends remain an obligation.

Meanwhile, Tether CEO Paolo Ardoino said the company holds around 150,000 bitcoin, CoinDesk reported on 3 November. Its latest reserve report implies roughly 100,060 BTC.

Those figures have different dates and potentially different scopes. Ardoino did not explain where the additional coins sit or when they were bought. This is not evidence of a fresh 50,000-BTC purchase.

The world’s largest digital-dollar issuer is saving in Bitcoin. Worth noticing. Worth reading carefully, too.

Make the useful change this week

Check who holds your keys, whether you could recover your wallet and whether a price drop would force you to sell. If those answers are vague, Virtuse Verdict can help identify gaps in your plan. It checks readiness, not whether Bitcoin is a buy.
