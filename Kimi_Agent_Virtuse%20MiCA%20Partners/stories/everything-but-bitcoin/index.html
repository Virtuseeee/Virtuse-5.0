Everything But Bitcoin

Bitcoin fell about 2.5% while the S&P hit a record weekly close, gold rose, and yields eased. The selling came from crypto’s own buyers.

By Ras Vasilisin Macro desk 12 Oct 2026 · 09:45 UTC Block — 4 min
Share

Bitcoin’s at about $83,000, down about 2.5% on the week.

The S&P 500 just posted its highest weekly close ever, gold climbed about 1.3% and the 10-year Treasury yield even finished a touch lower than it started, yet Bitcoin still went down.

Most of the selling came from crypto’s own buyers, and the reason they pulled back says a lot about where the US economy is right now and what has to change before they come back.

1. The Floor Gave Way For A Day

Last week we said Bitcoin was stuck in a range between roughly $82,600 and $87,000, and this week it tested both ends.

Early Monday it got turned back just shy of $87,000, the same area that has capped every rally since late September, and by Thursday it had slid to about $80,300, its lowest in almost three weeks.

By Friday it had climbed back to around $82,500, right at the bottom of the old range. By Sunday it was near $83,000.

US spot Bitcoin ETFs lost $485 million on Wednesday, their biggest one-day exit in the data we track, and another $244 million on Thursday.

Friday brought a small $21 million back in, but that still leaves the week about $679 million out by Farside’s count, and October roughly $386 million in the red.

Leverage made the slide worse. About $1.19 billion of positions were liquidated over 24 hours into Thursday night, more than $1 billion of it bets on prices going up. (A liquidation is an exchange force-closing a borrowed bet because the trader’s collateral has run out.)

The rest of the crypto market fell harder than Bitcoin, which is what usually happens when leverage unwinds.

2. Two Economies, One Price

To see why buyers are so thin in a week stocks hit a record, you have to look at who’s doing well in this economy.

The answer is people who already own a lot of stocks.

On Friday, the University of Michigan’s consumer sentiment index fell to 46.3. That is the second-lowest reading ever. Its measure of how people feel about their finances today hit a new record low.

Yet spending holds up. The top 20% of US households by income account for around 40% of all consumer spending, according to Bureau of Labor Statistics data cited by ING. The bottom 60% hold only about 15% of household wealth.

Economists call this a K-shaped economy. (One group goes up, the other goes down, like the two arms of the letter K.)

The top arm owns the index funds. When the S&P 500 makes a record, their wealth makes a record too. They have no reason to sell and no urgent reason to add something new.

The bottom arm is paying more for fuel and more for loans. 87% of people in the survey say it is a bad time to buy a home. That is the group that used to buy Bitcoin a few hundred dollars at a time, and right now it has less to spare.

3. The Price Of Money Went Up

The second reason is rates.

On Wednesday the Fed published the minutes of its September meeting, where it raised rates by a quarter point to 3.75% to 4%. Most officials said another hike before year end is likely.

The 10-year Treasury yield ended the week at 5.24%. (A yield is what the US government pays to borrow for ten years.) When a safe bond pays over 5%, holding an asset that pays nothing has a real cost, and short-term traders feel it first.

So the buyers who are flush already have what they want, and the buyers who want Bitcoin most have less to spend and more expensive money.

That is why Bitcoin fell in a week when everything else rose.

4. Who Bought Anyway

Not everyone left.

On-chain data shows larger wallets bought about 15,000 BTC in the three days after Wednesday’s drop, roughly $1.24 billion. (On-chain means visible on Bitcoin’s public ledger.)

On the other side, miner MARA moved 996 BTC, about $81 million, to a Galaxy Digital wallet, reportedly to help pay down debt.

Patient money bought. Borrowed money sold.

5. What Has To Change Before They Come Back

Three things.

First, yields. A 10-year yield moving back toward 5% or below would make holding Bitcoin cheaper again.

Second, ETF flows. One green day is not a trend. A run of inflow days would tell us the larger buyers are back.

Third, the bottom of the K. Lower fuel prices or better wage growth would put spare money back in the pockets of everyday savers.

None of this needs to happen all at once. But until at least one of them does, Bitcoin is likely to keep reacting to its own buyers, not to records in other markets.

6. What To Watch This Week

  • Monday 12 Oct: Columbus Day. US bond markets are closed, stocks trade.
  • Tuesday 13 Oct: Fed Governor Christopher Waller speaks.
  • Wednesday 14 Oct, 14:30 CEST: US consumer prices (CPI) for September. A hot number makes a December hike more likely.
  • Thursday 15 Oct: producer prices (PPI). Late that evening US time, Fed Chair Kevin Warsh talks with IMF chief Kristalina Georgieva.
  • Every day: ETF flows on Farside. Watch whether Friday’s small inflow turns into a streak.
  • The range: $82,600 is now the line to hold. $87,000 is still the ceiling.

The next Fed decision is on 28 October.
