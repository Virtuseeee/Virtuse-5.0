Stop Watching Bitcoin in Dollars. Watch It in Gold.

By Ras Vasilisin Macro desk 8 Nov 2026 · 21:16 UTC Block — 4 min
Share

I don’t have a good answer to what bitcoin will cost next month. And I’m slightly uneasy about how many people do.

What interests me more is whether one bitcoin buys more than it did a year ago. Not just more dollars. More of something that a central bank can’t create at a meeting.

Gold, for example.

That’s what Jurrien Timmer, Director of Global Macro at Fidelity, is looking at. In a post on X he wrote:

I still prefer Bitcoin over gold for the time being, and its relative price is holding up even as both assets have sold off.

— Jurrien Timmer (@TimmerFidelity) November 7, 2026

Sounds odd at first. Look at their ratio, and it starts making sense.

First, swap dollars for ounces

Most people open the BTC/USD chart. Green feels good. Red ruins the mood. Then someone explains why the move was entirely predictable. Always after the fact, naturally.

The bitcoin-to-gold ratio asks a more useful question: how many ounces of gold does one bitcoin buy?

In Timmer’s chart, that ratio has risen from roughly 12 ounces at the cycle low to around 21. An increase of about 75%. (You can track the live BTC/gold ratio and historical comparisons on Virtuse.)

That’s not a 75% dollar return. It means one bitcoin buys that much more gold than it did at that particular low.

The distinction matters.

Bitcoin can fall in dollars while gaining ground against gold. If you’re comparing the two assets, a dollar chart alone won’t tell you that.

It isn’t a buy signal, though. And it certainly doesn’t prove bitcoin will outperform gold every year from now on.

Then follow the money

Timmer’s argument goes beyond the chart.

The U.S. spot bitcoin ETFs have attracted nearly $55 billion in net inflows since 2024. The comparable figure for the gold and silver products discussed sits at around $29 billion.

A substantial gap.

But the detail matters: these are flows into particular investment products, not a comparison of the entire bitcoin market with the entire gold market.

Still, it tells us something.

Traditional investors no longer need an exchange they don’t trust and a wallet they don’t understand to get bitcoin exposure. An ETF lets them buy it in the same account they use for stocks.

That removes a barrier. It doesn’t remove the risk.

And the exit is just as convenient. Bitcoin ETFs saw roughly $412 million in outflows on Friday, November 6, and about $1.28 billion over the full week ending that day.

Institutional money isn’t a promise that nobody will sell again. It’s money. When its owners need it somewhere else, it leaves.

More upside doesn’t mean a safer investment

Timmer describes bitcoin as a higher-beta version of gold. In financial language, that means greater sensitivity to market moves.

For anyone who’d rather skip the jargon: bigger swings. Both ways.

Bitcoin and gold share a scarcity argument, but they aren’t interchangeable. Gold has a long history as a monetary asset, and central banks buy it. Bitcoin has a predetermined issuance schedule, a finite supply and the ability to move value without shipping metal around the world.

Those features can be reasons to own bitcoin. They aren’t reasons to ignore your purchase price or the size of your position.

If a downturn forces you to sell because you need the money for everyday expenses, a long-term thesis won’t help much. The thesis might be right. Your position was still too big.

Check whether the protection works during a sell-off

Here’s where the comfortable story gets awkward.

There is a correlation of 60–70% between both assets and the S&P 500. Without a precise measurement period and methodology, I wouldn’t turn that into a permanent rule. But it’s a reminder that neither asset is guaranteed to rise during the next stock-market sell-off.

When investors need cash, they sell things they still believe in.

So I separate two questions. Can bitcoin preserve value over the long term? And will it protect my portfolio next week?

A yes to the first doesn’t automatically give you a yes to the second. That distinction gets lost far too easily in enthusiastic investment discussions.

Watch the evidence, not who wins the argument

Timmer favouring bitcoin doesn’t mean he’s written off gold. He compares them and adjusts his view as conditions change. That makes more sense to me than defending one asset at any cost.

The BTC/gold ratio deserves a place beside the dollar chart. It shows whether bitcoin is gaining purchasing power against gold or losing it. It won’t do your risk management for you, but it can keep your enthusiasm honest.

And Timmer? His argument deserves attention. Even someone at Fidelity can’t turn a probability into a certainty.

Bitcoin doesn’t have to rise tomorrow to deserve your attention today. But if you’re buying it as a store of value, watch what it buys, not just the number beside the dollar sign.
