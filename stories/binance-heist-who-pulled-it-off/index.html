The $6.3 Billion Binance Heist: Who Actually Pulled It Off?

By Ras Vasilisin Treasury desk 1 Nov 2026 · 14:52 UTC Block — 3 min
Share

Six point three billion dollars vanished from Binance in about twenty minutes.

That is not a typo. It is the biggest crypto theft ever recorded.

Naturally, the internet lost its collective mind. People started connecting dots, spinning conspiracy theories, and pointing fingers. When a hole this big opens up, everyone wants to know who drove the truck.

Let’s break down the three main theories floating around about who was really behind the hack.

1. The Inside Job

This theory blew up after a viral thread suggested Binance faked the whole thing because it was secretly broke.

The logic? The hacker moved money across ten different blockchains in minutes, took a brutal ten percent haircut on Tether, and split the Bitcoin into neat little piles. Skeptics pointed out that it looked less like an attack and more like an internal stress test or a staged bank run.

The reality: The on-chain data doesn’t back this up. Yes, the thief got deep inside Binance’s systems and hijacked its hot-wallet signing machine. But if Binance wanted to stage a fake hack, inviting the FBI, the Secret Service, and global stablecoin issuers to freeze billions of dollars is a terrible way to hide a hole in your balance sheet. It’s like setting your own house on fire to check the smoke detectors.

2. The Outside Hacker

Another popular angle points to a sophisticated, highly targeted outside attack.

The official story started with a compromised laptop belonging to a single engineer. That breach gave the attacker access to a backend service container, letting them bypass normal approval controls and wipe out tracking tools as they went.

The reality: This wasn’t script-kiddie stuff. The attackers ran dry-run test transactions hours before the strike. They used Ethereum permission slips to trick the system into emptying a massive multi-chain register. Whether the entry point was a phishing link or bad internal security, an external actor executed a surgical strike.

3. North Korea (Lazarus Group)

Major blockchain analysis firms like TRM Labs and Chainalysis have pointed the finger in one specific direction: North Korea’s state-sponsored hacking units.

The stolen funds immediately began moving through patterns and pools tied to previous high-profile exchange hacks widely attributed to Pyongyang.

The reality: North Korea has a well-documented playbook of infiltrating crypto companies using elaborate social engineering, fake job interviews, and malware deployment. They specialize in massive, patient, cross-chain heists. When you look at the sheer scale and coordination required to pull off a multi-billion-dollar sweep across ten chains, state-level backing starts to look very likely.

What Happened to Bitcoin’s Price

When the drain started, Bitcoin was trading near $81,000. Within an hour it slipped under $80,000 and bottomed at about $76,400, its weakest level in six weeks. Roughly $1.5 billion in leveraged positions were liquidated within 24 hours, almost 90% of them longs, according to CoinGlass.

This was not Bitcoin failing. It was leverage failing, again.

We have seen this movie before. After Mt. Gox in 2014, Bitcoin roughly halved, yet it reached $19,700 by the end of 2017. After the FTX collapse in 2022, it fell about 22%, then set a new all-time high of $73,700 in March 2024. After the Bybit hack in 2025, it dropped about 5% and hit a new all-time high three months later. Every exchange hack has hit the price. None of them has stopped Bitcoin. Past performance guarantees nothing, but the pattern is hard to ignore.

The Real Takeaway

Was it an inside job? The thief certainly got inside the machinery to pull the trigger. But did Binance rob itself? The evidence says no.

To be clear, Bitcoin itself was not hacked: the network never missed a block, and once again it was an exchange that failed.

The attackers took 18,270 BTC. That sounds enormous, but it is less than 3% of the roughly 691,000 BTC Binance holds for its users, and less than 0.1% of all Bitcoin in existence.

The real issue isn’t just figuring out which hacker or nation-state won the loot. The scariest part is that three hundred million people had to sit around waiting for a corporate press release and a Monday deadline to find out if their money was safe.

When your savings depend on a centralized middleman, a freeze button, and a proof-of-reserves report, you aren’t holding crypto. You’re just hoping the bouncer lets you keep your own cash.
